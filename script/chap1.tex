\section{Two-Dimensional System}
\label{sec:twodim}
\subsection*{Lagrangian Equation of Motion}

\begin{marginfigure}
    \centering
    \include{figures/2dramp}
    \caption{A sketch of the considered system. The vector $\mathbf{m}|_0$ denotes the magnetic moment at $t=0$. One can see that the angle between $\mathbf{m}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ at any point in time is the difference between $\beta$ and $\theta$. Since $\cos$ is symmetric on $\mathbb{R}$, the change of sign as $\mathbf{m}$ passes $\mathbf{B}$ is not of concern.} 
    \label{fig:2dramp}
\end{marginfigure}
We start our investigation by considering a very simplified model of a rigid two-dimensional disk of mass $M$ with a constant magnetic moment $\mathbf{m}$ rolling on an inclined plane of length $l$ and angle $\alpha$ without slipping in the earth magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$. The moment of inertia is $I=\frac{MR^2}{2}$. Similar to the example of semi-holonomic constraints, our generalized coordinates are the rolling distance $q$ and the rotational angle $\theta$. Note that while $\mathbf{m}$ is constant in the rolling frame of the disk, the angle between $\mathbf{m}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ changes with the rolling motion. We have again the same semi-holonomic constraint 
\[
dq-Rd\theta = 0
.\] This is a first integral, yielding \[
q(t) - q_0= R\theta(t) - \theta_0 
\] where we set $q_0:=q(0)$ and $\theta_0 := \theta(0)$. For simplicity, we choose the coordinates in such a way that w.l.o.g. $q_0=0=\theta_0$. The kinetic energy is given by
\[
    T(q, \dot{q}) = \frac{M}{2} \|\dot{q}\|^2 + \frac{I}{2} \|\dot{\theta}\|^2 = \frac{3M}{4} \dot{q}^2
\] where we substituted $\theta = \frac{q}{R}$. Two potentials act on the disk, the first being gravity
\[
    U_\text{g}(q) = Mgh = (q-l)Mg \sin \alpha
.\] The second is due to the magnetic moment of the disk interacting with the earth's magnetic field. Assuming an ideal magnetic dipole interaction, the potential is governed by the angle between $\mathbf{m}$ and $\mathbf{B}$. 
\marginnote{Allowing $\beta$ to be arbitrary is probably not necessary for the practical experiment as the dipole will align itself with the outer field if one does not impair this movement until the disk starts rolling.}
Assuming an initial angle of $\beta$ at $t=0$, the angle at time $t$ is simply $\beta - \theta$. Therefore, the electromagnetic potential is
\[
    U_\text{em}(q)=- \langle \mathbf{m}, \mathbf{B} \rangle = -mB\cos\left(\beta - \frac{x}{R}\right)
\] with $m=\|\mathbf{m}\|$ and $B=\|\mathbf{B}\|$.\\
We obtain the Lagrangian
\[
    \mathcal{L}(q,\dot{q})= T-U_\text{g}-U_\text{em}= \frac{3M}{4} \|\dot{q}(t)\|^2+ (l-q(t))Mg\sin \alpha + mB \cos\left(\beta - \frac{q(t)}{R}\right)
\] which gives rise to just one Lagrangian equation of the second kind:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \dot{q}}\right) - \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial q}= 0
\end{equation}
This yields the ODE \[
    \ddot{q}(t)= \frac{2g}{3} \sin \alpha  + \frac{2mB}{3MR}\sin\left(\beta - \frac{q}{R}\right) = f(q(t))
\] which is a second-order autonomous ODE. We already discussed how to solve such an equation. The first integral needed is
\begin{align*}
    \int_{q_0=0}^q f(\tau) \, d\tau &= \int_{q_0=0}^q \left( \frac{2g}{3}\sin \alpha + \frac{2mB}{3MR} \sin \left(\beta - \frac{q}{R} \right) \right)\, d\tau \\
                                  &= \frac{2g}{3}\sin(\alpha)q - \frac{2mB}{3M} \left( \cos\left(\beta - \frac{q}{R}\right)- \cos\beta\right)
\end{align*}
We make some abbreviations to keep everything readable. Define $A:= \frac{4g}{3} \sin \alpha$, $B := -\frac{4mB}{3M}$ and $C:=\frac{4mB}{3M} \cos \beta$. The implicit solution is then given by
\begin{equation}
t = \pm \int_0^{q(t)} \frac{1}{\sqrt{Aq - B \cos(\beta - \frac{q}{R}) + C}} \, dq.
\end{equation}
Unfortunately, this integral has no closed-form solution. The only option is to calculate the integral numerically, for example my means of a Taylor series, and then invert pointwise to obtain $q=q(t)$. We will focus on some aspects which can be derived in limiting cases.
\subsection*{Symmetries}
The derived Lagrangian is not very abundant in symmetries. As $\mathcal{L}$ is autonomous, the system admits time translation symmetry and hence conservation of energy. However, this is not very surprising as we did not enforce the rolling condition by means of a dissipative Lagrangian but instead as semi-holonomic integrable constraint. The variable $q$ does appear explicitly in the Lagrangian, so it can not be cyclic. The only non-trivial symmetry remains time symmetry. 

\subsection*{Conclusion}
While it is quite surprising that such a simple system does not admit a closed-form analytic solution, we gain some first insight in the general usefulness of the Lagrangian formalism to derive equations of motion. However, our hope to solve the more complicated three-dimensional system is very much reduced, expecially since the three-dimensional rolling constraint is not always semi-holonomic.

\section{Three-dimensional System}
\subsection{Euler-Lagrangian Mechanics}
We want to adress the more complicated problem of a three-dimensional, spherical, solid magnetic dipole moving on an inclined plane in earth's magnetic field. Following \cite{rundquist}, we are dropping the restriction of the plane being an incline and consider two-dimensional smooth submanifolds of $\mathbb{R}^2$ as subsurface for the rolling ball. The system is characterized fully by three coordinates $(q^1,q^2,q^3)\in \mathbb{R}^3$ describing the center-of-mass motion with respect to a fixed coordinate frame and three Euler angles $(\phi, \psi , \theta) \in T^3$ describing the rotation of the body coordinate system. Hence, the configuration space is given by \[
Q = \mathbb{R}^3 \times \SO(3) 
.\] Taking a path $t \mapsto (q^1(t),q^2(t),q^3(t), \phi(t), \psi(t), \theta(t))$, we obtain the linear kinetic energy
\[
    T_\text{lin} (\dot{q}^1, \dot{q}^2, \dot{q}^3)=\frac{M}{2} \|\dot{\mathbf{q}}\|^2 
\] and the rotational kinetic energy 
\begin{align*}
    T_\text{rot}(\phi, \psi, \theta, \dot{\phi}, \dot{\psi}, \dot{\theta}) &= \frac{I}{2} \|\boldsymbol{\omega}\|^2
    \end{align*} where we wrote $\dot{\mathbf{q}}:=(\dot{q}^1,\dot{q}^2,\dot{q}^3)$ and $\boldsymbol{\omega}:=(\omega_1, \omega_2, \omega_3)$ for brevity.\marginnote{
        In our case, we are interested in a very simple surface describing a ramp in the $(q^1,q^2)$-plane. The most naive way to describe this is to restrict the configuration space to be the right half-space \[
        \mathbb{R}_+^3 = \left\{(x^1,x^2,x^3) \in \mathbb{R}^3 \mid x^2 \geq 0\right\} 
        .\] Then, we can define a continuous function $f: \mathbb{R}^3_+ \to \mathbb{R}$ given by \[
        f(q^2) := \begin{cases}\alpha q^2 + \beta &q^2 > \frac{\beta}{\alpha}\\0 &q^2 \leq \frac{\beta}{\alpha}\end{cases}
        \] where $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$ is the slope and $\beta \in \mathbb{R}$ is the maximal hight of the ramp. Clearly, this $f$ cannot be smooth at $q^2 = \frac{\beta}{\alpha}$ which will be a problem if we have to take directional derivatives later. While an ideal ramp will always suffer from a non-differentiable line parallel to the $q^1$-axis, we can replace it by the smooth function
        \[
            f_\delta(q^2):=\begin{cases}-\frac{\alpha x-\beta}{1-\exp(-\delta(\alpha x-\beta))} &q^2\neq \frac{\beta}{\alpha}\\ \frac{1}{\alpha} &q^2=\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\end{cases}
        ,\] where for $\delta \to \infty$, we recover the ideal ramp with slope $\alpha$ and $q^2$-intersection $\beta$.
    } Assume that the hypersurface restricing the motion of the ball in $q^3$-direction is given by an implicit equation \[
f(q^1(t), q^2(t))=q^3(t)
.\] Then, the gravitational potential is
\[
    U_\text{grav}(q^1,q^2)=M \cdot g \cdot f(q^1(t), q^2(t))
.\]
The magnetic moment of the ball is a vector $\mathbf{m} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ with $m := \|\mathbf{m}\|$ fixed in the rotating body frame but rotating from the view of the stationary frame. The magnetic field of the earth is a constant vector $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ with $B:=\|\mathbf{B}\|$. Assuming ideal magnetic dipole interaction, we need the scalar product $\langle \mathbf{m}, \mathbf{B} \rangle$ to get an expression for $U_\text{em}$ in Euler angles. For this, choose $(\phi_0, \psi_0, \theta_0)$ such that the body $z$-axis is aligned with the magnetic moment: $\mathbf{z} \perp \mathbf{m}$. Using that Euler angles are a local parametrization of $\SO(3)$, we can obtain the direction of the rotating $z$-axis at time $t$ by the action of the corresponding rotation matrix on $\mathbf{z}|_{t=0}$. The scalar product then decomposes into the easier form $mB\langle A_t(\phi, \psi, \theta) \mathbf{z}_{t=0}, \mathbf{B} \rangle$ with $A_t$ being a one-parameter subgroup corresponding to the chosen path, and we obtain
\begin{align*}
    U_\text{em} (\phi,\psi,\theta)&= - \langle \mathbf{m}, \mathbf{B} \rangle= - mB \langle A_t \mathbf{z}_{t=0}, \mathbf{B} \rangle\\
                                  &= -m(c_\psi s_\theta B_1+ s_\psi s_\theta B_2 + c_\theta B_3) = U_\text{em}(\psi, \theta).
\end{align*} 
The rolling constraint yields \[
    \|\boldsymbol{\omega}\| = \frac{\|\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}\|}{R}
\] and hence we can simplify \[
T_\text{rot}(\boldsymbol{q})=\frac{I}{2R^2} \|\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}\| 
,\]  eliminating the dependence on the Euler angles.\marginnote{
    Note that while we can eliminate $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ completely, we may need to recover this vector if we aspire to animate the results. While we could just solve for the Euler angles, it is easiert to note that the rolling condition also guarantees that \[
        \dot{\mathbf{q}} \perp \boldsymbol{\omega} \perp \mathbf{n}
    ,\] where $\mathbf{n}$ is the surface normal. Defining an auxilliary function \[
    F(\mathbf{q}):= q^3-f(q^1,q^2)
    \] which is the implicit equation of the surface, we can obtain the normal as \[
    \mathbf{n} = \frac{\grad F}{\| \grad F \|}
.\] With this, $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ can be fully reconstructed from the cartesian part of the configuration space:
\[
    \boldsymbol{\omega} = \frac{\|\dot{\mathbf{q}}\|}{R} \left( \frac{\grad F}{\|\grad F\|} \times \dot{\mathbf{q}} \right)
.\] 
} Using $I=\frac{2MR^2}{5}$ for an ideal ball, we can denote the Lagrangian as 
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{q}, \dot{\boldsymbol{q}}, \psi, \theta) &= U_\text{grav} + U_\text{em} - T_\text{kin} - T_\text{rot} \\
                                                                        &= Mgf(q^1, q^2) + -m(c_\psi s_\theta B_1 + s_\psi s_\theta B_2 + c_\theta B_3) - \frac{7M}{10} \|\dot{\boldsymbol{q}}\|^2
\end{split}
\end{equation}
This yields five Lagrangian equations of the second kind:
\begin{align}
    0 &= \frac{7M}{5}\dot{q}^1 + Mg \partial_1 f = \frac{7M}{5} \dot{q}^1\\
    0 &= \frac{7M}{5}\dot{q}^2 + Mg \partial_2 f \approx \frac{7M}{5}\dot{q}^2 + \alpha\\
    0 &= \frac{7M}{5}\dot{q}^3\\
0 &= m(-s_\psi s_\theta B_1 + c_\psi s_\theta B_2)\\
    0 &= m(c_\psi s_\theta B_1 + s_\psi c_\theta B_2 - s_\theta B_3) 
\end{align}
where we took the limit $\delta \to \infty$ in $f$ away from the non-differentiable points.\\
Unfortunately, the high non-linear nature of the equations makes simulations very difficult, so we considered another approach.
\subsection{Non-Holonomic Mechanics: The Rigid Ball}
We follow \cite[Bloch]. Consider a perfect homogeneous solid ball of mass $M$, radius $R$, and inertia scalar $I=\frac{2}{5}MR^2$ rolling without slipping on an incline of slope $\alpha \in \mathbb{R}$. Write $\bm{r} = (x,y,z)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^3$ for the bodies inertial coordinates and $P \in \SO(3)$ for the rotational coordinates, $\bm{\Omega}=(\Omega_1,\Omega_2,\Omega_3)^\top$ for the bodies inertial rotational velocity, and $\bm{\omega} = (\omega_1,\omega_2,\omega_3)^\top$ for the rotational velocity in the body frame. Furthermore, given a vector $\bm{v}=(v_1,v_2,v_3)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^3$, we define the associated cross-product operator  \[
    \hat{v}: \mathbb{R}^3 \to \mathbb{R}^3
\] as matrix operator \[
\marginnote{Note that this matrix is skew-symmetric. Strictly speaking, this operator arises by the isomorphism $\so(3) \cong \mathbb{R}^3$.}
\hat{v}:=
\begin{pmatrix}
    0 & -v_3 & v_2 \\ v_3 & 0 & - v_1\\ -v_2 & v_1 & 0
\end{pmatrix}
.\]
Denote by $(\bm{e}_1,\bm{e}_2,\bm{e}_3)$ the canonical Euclidean basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$. Our goal is to derive analytical equations of motion for $\bm{r}(t)$ and $P(t)$ under the influence of gravity $U=Mgz$. The incline is described as a zero set of $F(y,z)=z-\alpha y$, yielding the surface normal field \[
    \bm{n}= \frac{\grad F}{\|\grad F\|} = \frac{(0,-\alpha,1)}{\sqrt{\alpha^2+1}}
.\] 
The Lagrangian of this system is evidently given as
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}=T-U=\frac{M}{2} \|\dot{\bm{r}} \|^2 + \frac{I}{2} \|\bm{\Omega}\|^2 - Mgz.
\end{equation}
The virtual displacements are $\delta \bm{r}$ and $\delta P$. Since a virtual rotation is induced by the cross product on $\so(3)$, we can introduce the virtual rotation vector $\delta \bm{\theta}$ such that $\delta P = \widehat{\delta \bm{\theta}}(P)$. It is very useful to split the rotational degree of freedoms into parts normal and tangential to the incline. Write \[
    \marginnote{Physically, this reflects that we expect the rotation around the axis normal to the plane to be conserved as it does not influence the rolling. One can also think about this in terms of Noethers' Theorem since symmetry in this direction is unbroken.}
    \bm{\Omega} = \Omega_\perp \bm{n} + \bm{\Omega_\|}
    \] with $\Omega_\perp = \langle \bm{\Omega},\bm{n}  \rangle$ and $\bm{\Omega}_\| = \bm{\Omega} - \Omega_\perp \bm{n}$. The same splitting also applies to the angular variation \[
    \bm{\delta \theta} = \delta \theta_\perp \bm{n}+\bm{\delta \theta}_\|
.\] Since the ball rolls without slipping, we get the constraint \[
\dot{\bm{r}}=R \bm{\Omega} \times \bm{n}
.\] 
This implies that the admissible variations are resticted by \[
    \delta \bm{r} = R \delta \bm{\theta} \times \bm{n} = R (\delta \theta_\perp \bm{n} + \delta \bm{\theta}_\|) \times \bm{n} = R \delta \bm{\theta}_\| \times \bm{n}  = R \widehat{\delta \bm{\theta}_\|}(\bm{n})
.\]
With this, we can either do a variation of $\mathcal{L}$ or use the d'Alembertian principle directly since the forces are easily derived as \[
    \bm{F}_r=-M \ddot{\bm{r}} - Mg\bm{e}_3
\] and
\[
    \bm{F}_\theta=-I \dot{\bm{\Omega}}
.\] 
Now we calculate virtual work under the assumption of d'Alembert's principle:
\begin{align*}
    \marginnote{We used $\langle \bm{a},\bm{b} \times \bm{c}  \rangle = \langle \bm{b},\bm{c} \times \bm{a}  \rangle$ and symmetry of the scalar product. }
    0 &= \delta W= \langle \bm{F}_r,\delta \bm{r}  \rangle + \langle \bm{F}_\theta, \delta \bm{\theta}  \rangle = \langle -M(\ddot{\bm{r}} + g\bm{e}_3),\delta \bm{r}  \rangle -\langle I \dot{\bm{\Omega}},\delta \bm{\theta}  \rangle \\
      &= \langle -M(\ddot{\bm{r}}+g\bm{e}_3),R\delta \bm{\theta} \times \bm{n}  \rangle - \langle I \dot{\bm{\Omega}},\delta \bm{\theta}  \rangle \\
      &= \langle MR\bm{n} \times (- \ddot{\bm{r}}-g\bm{e}_3) -I\dot{\bm{\Omega}},\delta \bm{\theta}  \rangle 
\end{align*}
We know that the scalar product is non-degenerate, so for arbitrary $\delta \bm{\theta}$, it can only vanish if the first argument vanishes. This yields \[
    I\dot{\bm{\Omega}}= MR\bm{n} \times (-\ddot{\bm{r}}-g\bm{e}_3)
.\] Differentiating the rolling constraint, we obtain \[
\ddot{\bm{r}}=R \dot{\bm{\Omega}} \times \bm{n} + R \bm{\Omega} \times \dot{\bm{n}}=R \dot{\bm{\Omega}} \times \bm{n}
.\] Plugging in yields \[
\marginnote{We used \[
        (\bm{a} \times \bm{b}) \times \bm{c} = -\langle \bm{c},\bm{b}  \rangle \bm{a} + \langle \bm{c},\bm{a}  \rangle \bm{b}  
\] and $\dot{\Omega}_\perp \bm{n} - \dot{\bm{\Omega}} = \dot{\bm{\Omega}}_\|$. }
I \dot{\bm{\Omega}} = RM\bm{n} \times (R \dot{\bm{\Omega}} \times \bm{n}+g\bm{e}_3) = -R^2M \dot{\bm{\Omega}}_\| - RMg \bm{n} \times \bm{e}_3 
.\] 
We define projection operators $\pi_\perp=\bm{n} \bm{n}^\top$ onto $\spn\{\bm{n}\}$ and $\pi_\|=\id - \bm{nn}^\top$ onto $\spn\{\bm{n}\}^\perp$. The tangential equation reads \[
    I \pi_\| \dot{\bm{\Omega}}=I\dot{\bm{\Omega}}_\| = -R^2M\dot{\bm{\Omega}}_\| - RMg \bm{e}_3 \times \bm{n}
.\] Rearranging yields \[
\dot{\bm{\Omega}}_\| = \frac{RMg}{I+R^2M} \bm{n} \times \bm{e}_3
.\] 
This means that angular velocity parallel to the incline changes with a constant factor determined by the incline and the sphere's parameters. The normal equation is \[
    \pi_\perp \dot{\bm{\Omega}}=\dot{\Omega}_\perp = 0
,\] so the angular velocity normal to the incline is conserved. Substituting back into the rolling constraint, we obtain \[
\ddot{\bm{r}}=R\dot{\bm{\Omega}} \times \bm{n} = \frac{RMg}{I+R^2M} (\bm{e}_3-\langle \bm{n}, \bm{e}_3 \rangle \bm{n} )
.\] Hence the linear acceleration is constant, the sphere moves like a mass point at the center of gravity. Collecting everything, we have the complete solution
\begin{equation}
    \left.\begin{cases}
            \ddot{\bm{r}} = \frac{R^2 Mg}{I+R^2M}(\bm{e}_3 - \langle \bm{n},\bm{e}_3  \rangle \bm{n} )\\
    \dot{P}(t)= \widehat{\Omega}(P(t))\\
    \dot{\bm{\Omega}}_\| = \frac{RMg}{I+R^2M}(\bm{n} \times \bm{e}_3) \\
    \dot{\bm{\Omega}}_\perp = 0\\
    \bm{\omega} = P(t)^\top\bm{\Omega}
    \end{cases}. 
\right\}
\end{equation}
For the case at hand, we can restrict $\alpha \in [0,\frac{\pi}{2})$ and substitute $\alpha = \tan(\varphi)$, so $\bm{n}=\frac{(0,-\tan(\varphi),1)^\top}{\sec(\varphi)}=(0, -\sin(\varphi), \cos(\varphi))^\top$, yielding
\begin{equation*}
    \left.\begin{cases}
            \ddot{\bm{r}} = \frac{5g}{7}(0, \sin (\varphi) \cos (\varphi), 1-\cos(\varphi))^\top\\
        \dot{\bm{\Omega}}_\| = -\frac{5g\sin(\varphi)}{7}\bm{e}_1 \\
    \dot{\Omega}_\perp = 0\\
    \bm{\omega} = P^\top(t)\bm{\Omega}\\
    \dot{P}(t)= \widehat{\bm{\Omega}}(P(t))
    \end{cases} 
\right\}.
\end{equation*}
We see that $\bm{r}(t)$ is determined by a second-order ODE and $\Omega$ is determined by a first-order ODE, so we are free to choose three vector-valued initial conditions $\bm{r}_0, \bm{v}_0, \bm{\Omega}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^3$. The initial value problem is then uniquely solvable by Picard-Lindelöf's Theorem with solution 
\begin{equation}
    \left.\begin{cases}
            x(t)=v_{x,0}t+x_0\\
            y(t)=-\frac{5g}{14}\sin(\varphi)\cos(\varphi)t^2+v_{y,0}t+y_0\\
            z(t)=\frac{5g}{14}(1-\cos(\varphi))t^2+v_{z,0}t+z_0\\
            \Omega_1(t)=- \frac{5g}{7}\sin(\varphi)t + \Omega_{1,0}\\
            \Omega_2(t)= \Omega_{2,0}\\
            \Omega_3(t)=\Omega_{3,0}=:s
    \end{cases} 
\right\}.
\end{equation}
In the case where all initial values are chosen to vanish, we see that motion is governed by gravitational force in the same way as it is for a point mass. Rotation is only caused by gravitational acceleration in the corresponding direction, while all other rotations are conserved.
\paragraph*{Versor Representation}
For computational reasons, we want to convert rotational matrices to versors. Instead of a rotational matrix $P \in \SO(3)$, we describe the orientation now by a versor $\bm{q} \in \mathbb{S}^3$ and identify all vectors $\bm{v} \in \mathbb{R}^3$ with $(0, \bm{v}) \in \Im \mathbb{H} \subset \mathbb{H}$, their corresponding pure quaternion. We obtain:
\begin{equation*}
    \marginnote{Note that we made heavy use of $(0,\bm{v})(0,\bm{w})=(-\langle \bm{v},\bm{w}  \rangle, \bm{v} \times \bm{w})$.}
    \left.\begin{cases}
            (0,\ddot{\bm{r}}) = \frac{R^2 Mg}{I+R^2M}((0,\bm{e}_3) +\Re((0,\bm{n})(0,\bm{e}_3)) (0,\bm{n}) )\\
            \dot{\bm{q}}(t)= \frac{1}{2} (0,\bm{\Omega})\bm{q}\\
    \dot{\bm{\Omega}}_\| = \frac{RMg}{I+R^2M}(\Im((0,\bm{n}) (0,\bm{e}_3))) \\
    \dot{\bm{\Omega}}_\perp = 0\\
    \bm{\omega} = \Im(\bm{q}^{-1}(0,\bm{\Omega})\bm{q})
    \end{cases} 
\right\}.
\end{equation*}
\subsection{Non-Holonomic Mechanics: The Magnetic Sphere}
Keeping all notations and parameters from the previous subsection, we add a permanent magnetic dipole $\bm{\mu}\in \mathbb{R}^3$ to our solid sphere and introduce a constant homogeneous magnetic field $\bm{B} \in \mathbb{R}^3$. Note that there is now a subtle difference in the two frames: In the body frame, $\bm{\mu}$ is a constant vector revolving with the sphere, but the magnetic field moves from the perspective of the ball, so $\bm{B}_\text{body}(t)=P(t)^\top\bm{B}$. Viewing the situation from the inertial frame, $\bm{B}$ is constant, but $\bm{\mu}_\text{inert}(t)=P(t)\bm{\mu}$. We obtain a potential $U_\text{em}(t)=-\langle \bm{\mu},  \bm{B}\rangle$ and a new Lagrangian 
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}=T-U = \frac{M}{2}\|\dot{\bm{r}}\|^2+\frac{I}{2}\|\bm{\Omega}\|^2 - Mgz + \langle \bm{\mu},\bm{B}  \rangle.
\end{equation}
Again, we can skip the variational step and use Newton's law to obtain the new forces \[
    \bm{F}_r = -M \ddot{\bm{r}}-Mg\bm{e}_3
\] and
\[
    \bm{F}_\theta = -I \dot{\bm{\Omega}} + \bm{\mu} \times \bm{B}
.\] 
Our previous calculation still applies, so d'Alemberts principle yields
\[
    I\dot{\bm{\Omega}} = -R^2M \dot{\bm{\Omega}}_\| - RMg \bm{n} \times \bm{e}_3 + \bm{\mu} \times \bm{B}
.\] 
Projecting, we obtain the tangential equation 
\[
   I \dot{\bm{\Omega}}_\| = -R^2M\dot{\bm{\Omega}}_\| - RMg \bm{n}\times \bm{e}_3 + \pi_\|(\bm{\mu} \times \bm{B})
\] and the normal equation \[
I\dot{\bm{\Omega}}_\perp = \pi_\perp (\bm{\mu} \times \bm{B})
.\] 
Again, we obtain the linear acceleration
\[\marginnote{The normal part of $\dot{\bm{\Omega}}$ does not contribute since its cross product with $\bm{n}$ vanishes.}
    \ddot{\bm{r}}(t)=R\dot{\bm{\Omega}}\times \bm{n}= \frac{RMg}{I+R^2M} (\bm{e}_3 - \langle \bm{n},\bm{e}_3  \rangle \bm{n} ) + \frac{R}{I+R^2M} \pi_\|(\bm{\mu}\times \bm{B}) \times \bm{n}
.\] 
We can directly observe multiple things:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The linear motion only depends on the tangential part of the magnetic torque. If the torque is a scalar multiple of $\bm{n}$, the center-of-mass motion will be just as in the non-magnetic case, but the rotation may still be more complicated.
    \item The spin about the third axis is no longer conserved in general. It is only conserved if the torque is fully tangential to the plane.
    \item Even if the torque is fully tangential, the equations of motion become considerably more difficult as long as $\bm{\mu}$ and $\bm{B}$ enter as they are time-dependent, so accelerations are no longer constant and we do not expect the sphere to behave like a point mass at the centre of gravity.
\end{enumerate}
We do not expect the full equations to be analytically solvable since they are non-linear in the general case: The rotational matrices always introduce trigonometric terms via the torque. However, extreme cases may be of interest to understand the system. Also, the Lagrangian allows for analysis of symmetries by Noether's theorem. To consider this, we collect the full equations for our system in particular in the magnetic case, denoting the torque by $\bm{\tau}:=\bm{\mu} \times \bm{B}$ with tangential part $\bm{\tau}_\|$ and normal part $\tau_\perp$:
\begin{equation*}
    \left.\begin{cases}
            \ddot{\bm{r}} = \frac{5g}{7}(0, \sin (\varphi) \cos (\varphi), 1-\cos(\varphi))^\top + \frac{5\cos(\varphi)}{7RM}(\bm{\tau}_\| \times (0,-\alpha,1)^\top)\\
            \dot{\bm{\Omega}}_\| = -\frac{5g\sin(\varphi)}{7}\bm{e}_1 + \frac{5}{2RM} \bm{\tau}_\|\\
            \dot{\Omega}_\perp = \frac{5}{2R^2M} \tau_\perp\\
    \bm{\omega} = P^\top(t)\bm{\Omega}\\
    \dot{P}(t)= \widehat{\bm{\Omega}}(P(t))
    \end{cases} 
\right\}.
\end{equation*}
\paragraph*{The Normal Torque}
The first edge case is a fully normal torque $\bm{\tau}=\tau_\perp \bm{n}$. The parallel part vanishes, the centre-of-mass motion reduces to the non-magnetic case, as does the tangential rotation. The differential equations for $\ddot{\bm{r}}$ and $\dot{\bm{\Omega}}_\|$ remain analytically solvable, but we have to take a closer look at $\dot{\Omega}_\perp$. In the inertial frame, we have \[
    \bm{\tau}(t)=(P(t)\bm{\mu}) \times \bm{B}
.\] Applying the normal projection, we obtain \[
\tau_\perp= \langle ((P(t)\bm{\mu})\times \bm{B}), \bm{n} \rangle \bm{n}
.\]
